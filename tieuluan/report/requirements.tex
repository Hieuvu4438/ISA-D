\chapter{Requirements Identification}
\section{Business background}
Atelier Fashion sells clothing and accessories through an online storefront. Merchandising staff organise seasonal collections, categories, material descriptions, care instructions, photographs, and size charts. Each product design has one product identifier. Each sellable size/colour combination has one unique SKU. A blue shirt in size M and the same blue shirt in size L are distinct inventory items even when their listed prices match.

The retailer earns revenue from merchandise sales. Unlike a marketplace, it owns stock and does not calculate commissions or seller payouts. Garment suppliers deliver stock to the warehouse; shipping and payment providers perform external services. Customers need accurate fit and availability information, while internal staff require unambiguous item identities and evidence of what was promised at purchase.

\section{Proposed system, boundary, and objectives}
The proposed system integrates a public storefront, authenticated customer services, staff operations, and controlled provider adapters. Inside the boundary are catalogue publication, variant carts, checkout, stock reservations, order coordination, return decisions, refund requests, access control, and reporting. Outside are physical manufacture, parcel transport, the provider's settlement process, and supplier purchasing contracts. Provider status is recorded and verified rather than invented by the application.

The proposed objectives are to preserve exact SKU identity through each transaction, prevent committed stock from exceeding sellable stock, block fulfilment without verified payment, maintain traceable monetary events, and make post-purchase decisions auditable. Success is assessed against acceptance scenarios rather than unsupported claims of a percentage improvement over a nonexistent measured baseline.

\subsection{Feasibility and delivery approach}
A modular web application with a transactional relational database is technically appropriate for a single retailer because stock and order changes can share a local transaction. Hosted payment pages reduce the application's direct handling of payment credentials. Operational feasibility depends on staff agreeing on SKU naming, barcode practice, return inspection, and responsibility for exception queues. Economic feasibility cannot be proved without supplier quotations and transaction volumes; a later budget must include hosting, provider fees, support, security review, and staff training.

Delivery proceeds through catalogue/SKU cleanup, customer ordering and payment, warehouse workflows, then returns and reporting. Each phase requires approved requirements, migration reconciliation, and acceptance review. A pilot warehouse count establishes opening stock; spreadsheets are archived and reconciled before becoming read-only. No estimated implementation cost or achieved return on investment is asserted.

\section{Elicitation and validation plan}
The proposed elicitation methods are interviews with merchandising, warehouse, finance, and customer-service representatives; observation of receiving, picking, and returns; inspection of anonymised order sheets and payment references; and walkthroughs of difficult scenarios such as the last unit being purchased twice. Customer testing should focus on selecting unavailable sizes, understanding size charts, and distinguishing pending payment from confirmed purchase. These are planned activities, not completed research.

Open decisions include the chosen payment/carrier interfaces, excluded return categories, shipping fee rules, and retention schedules. Before development, the manager and relevant staff approve a requirement baseline. Changes receive a version, reason, impact assessment, and updated links to use cases, rules, data, and acceptance scenarios.

The fourth MoSCoW category, \emph{Won't in this release}, includes guest checkout, cash on delivery, marketplace seller onboarding/settlement, manufacturing, multi-warehouse allocation, and perishable expiry tracking. \section{Business rules and invariants}
\begin{longtable}{P{.1\linewidth}P{.8\linewidth}}
\caption{Business rule baseline}\label{tab:rules}\\
\toprule ID & Rule \\\midrule\endfirsthead
\toprule ID & Rule \\\midrule\endhead
BR01 & SKU is unique across the retailer. A product/size/colour tuple identifies at most one variant. Only published active variants are purchasable.\\
BR02 & Cart membership does not reserve inventory. For each SKU, available stock equals saleable on-hand stock minus active reserved stock; all three values are nonnegative.\\
BR03 & Checkout reserves every order line atomically or reserves none. The initial payment hold lasts 15 minutes. Expiry closes the order and releases active holds once; a late captured payment creates a compensation/refund exception rather than reviving the order.\\
BR04 & Unit prices, line discounts, SKU descriptions, recipient address, and delivery charge are snapshotted after customer consent. A changed quote requires renewed consent. Amounts use integer VND, not floating-point arithmetic.\\
BR05 & Order total equals the sum of quantity times agreed unit price less allocated line discount, plus delivery charge. A line discount cannot exceed its gross line value. Listed prices are treated as tax-inclusive in this academic case; tax compliance requires separate jurisdictional review.\\
BR06 & Payment is successful only after trusted provider verification of its reference, order, amount, and currency. A browser success page alone cannot authorise packing. Duplicate events have no duplicate business effect.\\
BR07 & Only a paid, confirmed order can enter packing. Customer cancellation is permitted only in PendingPayment or Confirmed. A version/lock check resolves cancellation versus packing so only one eligible transition succeeds.\\
BR08 & At dispatch, consume the reservation and reduce on-hand stock by the same quantity. Cancellation/expiry releases reserved stock without reducing on-hand stock. Once dispatched, use a return process rather than cancellation.\\
BR09 & Return requests must reference delivered order lines and be submitted within the assumed 14-day policy window. Completed and active return allocations together cannot exceed the quantity delivered for a line. Rejected/withdrawn allocations are released.\\
BR10 & Refund approval and returned-stock restoration are distinct events. Only received, inspected, saleable goods increase saleable on-hand stock. Damaged goods are recorded separately.\\
BR11 & An exchange replaces a line with another size/colour of the same product at the same agreed price. Staff allocate replacement stock after inspection before confirming the exchange; unavailable replacements require customer choice of refund or withdrawal.\\
BR12 & Confirmed plus pending refund commitments cannot exceed captured funds, and line refund commitments cannot exceed the refundable discounted value of eligible quantities. Delivery charge is refunded for full pre-dispatch cancellation or permanent non-delivery closure under BR15; post-delivery item returns exclude it. Partial item refunds use deterministic per-unit discount allocation.\\
BR13 & Payment, fulfilment, return, and refund status are separate. Delivered remains the fulfilment fact even when some items are returned or refunded. RefundRequested is not evidence that money has reached the customer.\\
BR14 & A staff role confers only its assigned permissions. Customers can read or change only their own resources; suppliers have no direct software access.\\
BR15 & Permanent non-delivery closure requires verified carrier evidence and Customer Service authorisation while the order is Shipped. It schedules a full captured-amount refund, including delivery. Restocking requires a unique physical carrier-return receipt and saleability inspection; lost goods are not restocked. Temporary delivery attempts do not trigger closure.\\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}
