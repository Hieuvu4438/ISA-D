1. Requirements Identification
1.1. Introduction
E-Commerce Marketplace is a multi-vendor online retail platform that connects consumers with inde-
pendent third-party merchants selling physical goods. Unlike a single-merchant online store where one
enterprise controls the catalogue, warehouse stock, and order fulfilment under a single roof, a multi-vendor
marketplace coordinates independent shops that set their own prices, maintain their own inventory counts,
and pack their own parcels. Every purchase therefore crosses organizational boundaries among the buyer,
the selling shop, internal platform staff, the payment gateway, and the shipping carrier.
This report investigates the operational structure of E-Commerce Marketplace and establishes the require-
ments and analysis models for its information system. Chapter 1 examines the business model, stake-
holder relationships, current operational bottlenecks, proposed system boundary, and functional and non-
functional requirements. Chapter 2 develops the actor and use case models, detailed use case specifi-
cations, activity workflows, transaction state lifecycles, domain concepts, analysis class diagrams, and
governing business rules.
1.2. Business Background
1.2.1. Organization and Business Model
E-Commerce Marketplace operates as a commercial intermediary rather than a traditional retailer. Instead
of buying stock from wholesalers and storing goods in a centralized warehouse, the platform provides
the digital infrastructure where independent Sellers register their own Shops, publish product listings,
manage stock availability, and prepare confirmed customer orders for carrier pickup. Internally, daily
platform operations are divided among four staff roles: the Administrator, Support Staff, Finance Staff,
and the Manager. Externally, the platform integrates with a Payment Service for online transactions and a
Shipping Provider for physical parcel delivery and cash-on-delivery (COD) collection.
Revenue is generated through transaction fees charged on completed orders. Because the marketplace
collects online payments or tracks COD collection on behalf of participating shops before disbursing net
earnings, financial accounting distinguishes the gross merchandise value (GMV) of ordered items, the
actual funds collected from the customer, the platform commission fee, and the net payout owed to the
Seller. Keeping these figures distinct allows staff to reconcile customer refunds, unpaid COD orders, and
seller settlements accurately.
1.2.2. Products, Customers, and Core Business Activities
The marketplace catalogue consists of everyday consumer products sold as discrete physical units. At
the domain level, each Product record has a platform-wide identifier (productId) and represents a specific
sellable listing and stock-keeping unit (sku) owned by a single Shop. Because SKU codes are assigned by
individual merchants, SKU uniqueness is enforced within each Shop through the composite key (shopId,
sku). If two shops sell the same commercial item, each shop maintains its own Product record with its
own price, description, and stock counts. Customers browse the catalogue by category and price or search
for specific products by keyword, voice, or image. Before committing to a purchase, a buyer needs clear
visibility into which shop is selling the item, whether stock is available, and what the final payable total
will be.
Operations across the marketplace flow through eleven connected activities: seller shop registration, cat-
alogue listing moderation, product discovery, shopping cart management, single-shop checkout, payment
processing, seller order packing, carrier shipping and delivery, post-purchase support and refunds, seller
financial settlement, and management reporting. A core distinction in this workflow separates shopping
intention from purchase commitment. Adding items to a cart records a buyer’s temporary selection across
one or more shops without holding inventory, whereas completing checkout creates a binding order for
one selected shop and reserves the corresponding stock.

1.4. Business Activity and Information Analysis
In the existing manual and spreadsheet-based operation, transaction data is fragmented across disconnected
files maintained by different teams:
- Catalogue moderation and customer profiles: Sellers submit new product listings and price changes
through shared spreadsheets, while the Administrator records approval decisions in a separate mod-
eration log. Because listing edits and approval states are not linked in a single database, unapproved
price changes or discontinued items frequently remain visible to shoppers. Similarly, customer deliv-
ery addresses are copied manually from profile sheets into order logs. When a buyer updates their
default profile address for a future purchase, staff inspecting the profile sheet can mistakenly overwrite
or misread the delivery address agreed for an earlier order still in transit.
- Order handling and inventory control: When a buyer submits an order, staff manually split multi-
shop selections into separate shop sheets and forward them to the respective Sellers. Stock counts are
deducted only when warehouse staff finish packing at the end of a shift rather than at the moment of
checkout. Consequently, two buyers can check out the last remaining unit of an item simultaneously,
forcing the Seller to cancel one of the confirmed orders. Conversely, when a buyer requests a refund,
staff sometimes add the refunded quantity back into available stock immediately upon approving the
request, before the Seller has received and inspected the returned parcel.

- Payment verification, shipping, and financial reporting: Online payment notifications from the
Payment Service and COD collection reports from the Shipping Provider are recorded in a single paid-
or-unpaid spreadsheet column. This simplified label cannot distinguish whether an online payment
timed out with an unknown outcome, whether COD cash has been collected by the courier, whether
a customer refund has been issued, or whether the Seller has already been paid. At the end of the

1.6. Proposed Information System
1.6.1. System Purpose and Scope
The proposed E-Commerce Marketplace Information System replaces fragmented spreadsheets with a
unified transactional platform that coordinates shops, moderated listings, inventory commitments, single-
shop orders, payments, shipments, support cases, refunds, and settlements:
- Single-shop ownership and SKU listings: Each Seller registers and operates at most one Shop (with a
recorded pickup address), while the marketplace hosts many independent Shops. Every Product record
has a global productId and a shop-scoped SKU code (shopId, sku) with its own unit price, available
stock, and committed stock.
- Multi-shop cart and single-shop checkout: A Customer can collect products from several shops in-
side one shopping cart, where lines are grouped visually by selling Shop. To keep fulfilment, shipping,
and settlement unambiguous, each checkout transaction creates one Order for one selected Shop group
while leaving items from other shops intact in the cart. Dynamic shipping fee calculation is outside the
current scope, so the order total equals the sum of confirmed item amounts (∑quantity×unitPrice).
- Payment, fulfilment, cancellation, and full-order refunds: Checkout supports both online payment
via the Payment Service and COD collected by the Shipping Provider. Each confirmed Order is fulfilled
by its owning Shop and shipped as a single parcel. Customers may cancel an order directly only before
the Seller hands the parcel over to the carrier. After handover, issues are handled through formal support
cases, and approved refunds are processed as full-order refunds of the collected total.
- Financial settlement and multi-modal discovery: Each Order links to at most one seller settlement
record once delivery and payment collection are verified. For product discovery, core keyword, cate-
gory, and price filtering are complemented by optional voice and image search.
1.6.2. System Boundary and Context Diagram
Inside the software boundary lie account management, shop onboarding, listing moderation, product
search, cart management, single-shop checkout, stock reservation, order and shipment tracking, payment
and COD status recording, support case resolution, full-order refunds, seller settlement, buyer reviews, and
period reporting. Outside the software boundary lie the external Payment Service and Shipping Provider
platforms, physical warehouse picking and packing performed by Sellers, external supplier procurement,
and the human stakeholders interacting with the system.

checkout confirmations, support requests, and product reviews to the system, and receives catalogue
listings, order confirmations, and separate payment and delivery statuses. On the supply side, the Seller
submits shop applications, SKU listings, stock adjustments, and carrier handover updates, and receives
moderation status notifications, own-shop orders, and settlement statements.
- Internal governance flows: The Administrator submits shop approval, listing moderation, and account
decisions while receiving shop applications, pending SKU listings, and the moderation queue. Support
Staff receive customer, order, payment, tracking, and evidence data to evaluate disputes and submit
dispute decisions, refund approvals, and support notes. Finance Staff receive order totals, payment
references, and payout ledger data and submit unknown payment reconciliations, refund executions,
and settlement records, while the Manager provides reporting period and filter criteria and receives
GMV, collected funds, refund, fee, and settlement reports.
- External service flows: The system sends payment requests, refund requests, and transaction refer-
ences to the Payment Service and receives verified payment results, refund outcomes, and transaction
statuses. For physical logistics, the system transmits shipment requests, shop pickup addresses, and
recipient address snapshots to the Shipping Provider and receives tracking updates, parcel delivery
statuses, and COD collection confirmations.
1.6.3. System Objectives
To resolve problems P1 through P6, the proposed system enforces six operational objectives: (1) expose
only administrator-approved product listings belonging to active shops, (2) recheck live availability and
reserve stock at checkout so two buyers cannot claim the same unit, (3) link every order to its owning
shop, payment reference, and carrier tracking code, (4) maintain independent state lifecycles for order
progress, online payment verification, COD cash collection, customer refunds, and seller payouts, (5)
attach every support case and full-order refund to fixed order snapshots while requiring seller confirmation
of returned goods before restocking, and (6) generate role-scoped reports that distinguish merchandise
value, cancellations, collected funds, refunds, platform fees, and net seller payouts.
1.7. Stakeholder Needs
Translating the system context into concrete requirements requires examining how concurrent operations
affect each stakeholder. In a live marketplace, two buyers frequently view the final unit of a popular
item at the same time. If stock is held as soon as an item is placed in a cart, abandoned carts quickly
Page 5
lock up a seller’s inventory. Conversely, if stock is deducted only after warehouse packing, concurrent
checkouts inevitably cause overselling. Moving stock from availableStock to committedStock at checkout
confirmation resolves both risks while preventing a Seller from manually reducing available stock below
quantities already promised to active orders.
A similar coordination challenge arises when an online payment request times out without a definitive
response from the Payment Service. Treating an unanswered timeout as a failed payment and allowing
an immediate retry risks charging the buyer twice if the initial request actually succeeded at the gateway.
Recording the payment in an explicit Unknown state keeps the order and its committed stock on hold while
Finance Staff reconcile the provider reference. The operational needs of each stakeholder group are paired
with their business drivers in Table 1.4.

1.9. Non-Functional Requirements
To ensure that functional workflows remain dependable under real marketplace traffic, the system must
satisfy twelve non-functional requirements (NFR01 to NFR12) spanning performance, security, usability,
availability, reliability, scalability, maintainability, and transactional data integrity:
- Search response performance (NFR01): The system shall return keyword and filter search results
within 3 seconds at the 95th percentile under a baseline load of 10,000 published listings and 50 con-
current users, and within 5 seconds at the 95th percentile under 20,000 listings and 100 concurrent
users. Should Have.
- Role and ownership access control (NFR02): The system shall enforce role-based and ownership-
based authorization so that a Customer can access only their own cart and orders, a Seller can manage
only their own Shop, SKUs, and orders, and staff can invoke only functions assigned to their role. Must
Have.
- Credential and payment data protection (NFR03): The system shall store account passwords exclu-
sively as salted cryptographic hashes and shall never store raw payment card numbers or security codes
inside marketplace tables. Must Have.
- Usability and validation feedback (NFR04): The system shall support full keyboard navigation
across search, cart, and checkout forms and display field-level validation messages beside any rejected
input. Should Have.
- Cross-device browser compatibility (NFR05): The system shall render search, cart, checkout, and
order tracking interfaces without horizontal clipping on standard desktop (1366 × 768) and mobile
(390×844) viewports in current Chrome and Edge browsers. Should Have.
- Operational availability and graceful degradation (NFR06): The system shall maintain at least 99%
uptime during scheduled operating windows (08:00 to 17:00 across five business days per week) and
keep keyword search, cart management, and COD checkout operational if voice recognition, image
matching, or the external online payment gateway is temporarily unreachable. Should Have.
- Transactional durability and recovery (NFR07): The system shall persist accepted orders, stock
commitments, and payment records in durable storage so that no committed transaction is lost across a
service restart. Must Have.
Page 8
- Idempotent callback handling (NFR08): The system shall process repeated checkout submissions,
payment callbacks, or carrier tracking updates carrying the same reference code without duplicating
charges, refunds, or stock changes. Must Have.
- Catalogue and traffic scalability (NFR09): The system shall scale from 10,000 to 20,000 published
listings and from 50 to 100 concurrent users within the 5-second response ceiling of NFR01 without
requiring schema changes to existing records. Should Have.
- Modular integration maintainability (NFR10): The system shall isolate external payment and ship-
ping gateway adapters behind clear service boundaries so that updating a provider interface does not
alter core order, inventory, or settlement rules. Should Have.
- Stock commitment integrity (NFR11): The system shall guarantee non-negative availableStock and
committedStock across all SKUs and prevent concurrent checkouts or manual seller edits from over-
committing the final available unit. Must Have.
- Order snapshot immutability and audit trail (NFR12): The system shall preserve confirmed order
item and address snapshots against subsequent catalogue or profile edits and log the acting user, times-
tamp, and reason for every moderation, stock adjustment, support, refund, and settlement action. Must
Have.
1.10. Requirement Prioritization
Grouping the requirements by delivery priority establishes a practical implementation sequence:
- Must Have: Core account registration, single-shop onboarding, listing moderation, keyword search,
multi-shop cart management, single-shop checkout with stock reservation and fixed snapshots, online
and COD payment recording, carrier tracking, and manual stock control (FR01 to FR21, together
with NFR02, NFR03, NFR07, NFR08, NFR11, and NFR12) form the minimum viable transactional
baseline. Without these safeguards, the platform cannot prevent overselling, cross-shop order mix-ups,
or duplicate payment charges.
- Should Have: Post-handover support case management, full-order refunds, seller settlement account-
ing, period reporting, verified buyer reviews, and quantitative service-level targets (FR22 to FR26,
alongside NFR01, NFR04 to NFR06, NFR09, and NFR10) complete the commercial and governance
lifecycle once core ordering is stable.
- Could Have: Voice-based query transcription and image-based product discovery (FR27 and FR28)
enrich buyer convenience without affecting core transaction processing if schedule or computing re-
sources are constrained.

2. Requirements Analysis
2.1. Actor Analysis
In Unified Modeling Language (UML) analysis, an actor represents a cohesive role played by a human
user or an external system when interacting with the subject boundary. Based on the stakeholder context
established in Chapter 1, the marketplace interacts with five primary actor categories:
- Customer: Represents both unauthenticated visitors who browse and search public listings and au-
thenticated buyers who manage their shopping cart, complete single-shop checkout, make payments,
track order progress, cancel eligible orders before carrier handover, raise post-purchase support claims,
and submit product reviews.
- Seller: Represents an authenticated merchant who registers at most one Shop, maintains product list-
ings and SKU stock levels for that Shop, packs confirmed orders and hands parcels to the carrier,
responds to customer support cases, and inspects own-shop sales and settlement statements.
- Platform Staff (Administrator, Support Staff, Finance Staff, and Manager): Represents the inter-
nal governance roles of the marketplace. The Administrator reviews shop applications and moderates
product listings, Support Staff investigate post-handover complaints and approve full-order refunds,
Finance Staff reconcile unknown online payments and record seller settlements, and the Manager gen-
erates operational and financial performance reports.
- Payment Service: Represents the external payment gateway that processes online payment requests
and refund instructions by transaction reference and returns verified outcomes.
- Shipping Provider: Represents the external logistics carrier that receives parcel pickup and recipient
details and returns tracking milestones, delivery confirmations, and COD cash collection updates.

2.4. Supporting Processes and Transaction States
2.4.1. Order Fulfilment, Cancellation, and Post-Purchase Support
Beyond the six primary workflows modeled above, the remaining use cases govern order fulfilment, pre-
handover cancellation, and post-purchase dispute resolution. In Process Orders (UC11), a Seller packs and
hands over an order only after it belongs to their Shop and has reached the Placed state, which indicates
either that online payment is verified as Paid or that a COD order is recorded with PendingCollection.
Recording carrier handover creates the Shipment record with its tracking code and updatedAt timestamp
and transitions the order to Shipped without deducting availableStock a second time.
Under Cancel Order (UC12), a Customer may cancel an order directly only while the order is in Await-
ingPayment, Placed, or Processing prior to carrier handover. A valid pre-handover cancellation transitions
the order to Cancelled and moves the reserved quantities from committedStock back to availableStock.
Once the Seller has handed the parcel to the Shipping Provider, direct cancellation is blocked and any
post-handover issue (such as damaged goods, wrong items, or delivery failure) must be submitted through
Resolve Support Case (UC15). Each SupportCase links directly to the Order snapshot, records the buyer’s
reason and evidenceReference, captures the Seller’s reply in sellerResponse, and stores Support Staff’s
final decision. When a claim is approved, the system creates a full-order Refund record for the collected
order total, while any physical goods returned by the buyer are added back to availableStock only after the
Seller confirms physical receipt and resale condition (setting returnInspected to true).
2.4.2. Financial Settlement and Period Reporting
Because dynamic shipping fee calculation is outside the system scope, an order’s payable total equals the
sum of its confirmed line-item amounts (∑quantity×unitPrice). When a full-order refund is approved, the
customer is refunded the collected order total in full. Under Manage Settlement (UC16), an order becomes
eligible for seller payout only when three conditions hold simultaneously: the parcel has reached the Deliv-
ered state, customer payment has been verified as collected (either online Paid or COD with codCollected
set to true), and no open blocking SupportCase remains unresolved. Finance Staff record a Settlement en-
Page 23
try capturing the grossAmount, platformFee, refundAmount, netAmount, status, and settledAt timestamp.
If an exceptional dispute results in a refund after a payout was already disbursed, Finance Staff record
an Adjusted settlement state rather than deleting historical payment records. Finally, in Generate Reports
(UC17), reports generated for the Manager, Administrator, or Seller specify the reporting period and break
down order totals, cancelled orders, collected funds, completed refunds, platform commission fees, and
net seller payouts.

2.6. Cart, Order, and Checkout Analysis
2.6.1. Structural Distinction Between CartItem and OrderItem
A central structural decision in the domain model separates temporary shopping selections (Cart and Car-
tItem) from binding purchase records (Order and OrderItem). Each Customer owns at most one active
Cart, which may be empty or hold multiple CartItems drawn from different Shops. Because the desired
Page 25
quantity is a property of a specific Product inside a specific Cart rather than a property of the Product itself,
CartItem is modeled as a distinct class linking Cart and Product. While a CartItem exists, it references the
live Product record to display current prices and availability grouped by Shop, leaving availableStock and
committedStock untouched.
Once the Customer selects one shop group and completes checkout, the system creates an Order and one
or more OrderItem instances for that Shop. Unlike a CartItem, an OrderItem is a permanent commercial
record. While retaining a traceability link to the underlying Product record, OrderItem copies the SKU
code, product name, and unit price into fixed snapshot attributes (skuSnapshot, nameSnapshot, and unit-
Price), and Order copies the confirmed delivery address into addressSnapshot alongside the payable total.
Consequently, if a Seller later changes a product’s price, edits its description, or discontinues the listing,
or if a Customer updates their defaultAddress on their profile, historical orders remain completely intact
for fulfilment, dispute resolution, and financial auditing. The structural differences between CartItem and
OrderItem are contrasted in Table 2.10.

2.6.2. Checkout Transformation
Checkout executes the state transformation that converts one selected shop group inside a Cart into a
persisted Order and its constituent OrderItem records. Suppose a Customer’s cart contains two units
of an item from Shop A priced at 150,000 VND per unit and one unit of an item from Shop B priced
at 300,000 VND. When the Customer invokes checkout(cart : Cart) : Order for Shop A, the system
verifies that Shop A is active, the listing is published, and at least two units of availableStock exist. Upon
confirmation, the system creates a new Order linked to Shop A with total equal to 300,000 VND and a
locked addressSnapshot (copied from the Customer’s defaultAddress or entered checkout address), creates
the corresponding OrderItem storing quantity = 2 and unitPrice = 150,000 VND, invokes commitStock(2)
on Shop A’s Product (reducing availableStock by 2 and increasing committedStock by 2), and removes
Shop A’s CartItem from the cart. Shop B’s CartItem remains in the cart for a future checkout.
