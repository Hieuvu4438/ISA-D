Chapter 1
REQUIREMENTS IDENTIFICATION
1.1
Introduction to the Problem
1.1.1
Business context
Fashion e-commerce differs from the sale of a single, undifferentiated item. One product
design can be offered in several colors and sizes; every combination must be identified,
priced, displayed, counted, picked, and recorded consistently. In the ABC Fashion case
described in the business investigation, variant information is fragmented, inventory up-
dates rely on spreadsheets, and size information does not sufficiently support customers’
choices. These conditions contribute to canceled orders, incorrect fulfillment, and size
exchanges.
This chapter identifies the business problems, stakeholders, needs, system boundary,
and requirements. The case is an academic analysis; no new field interviews, measured
outcomes, or operational statistics are claimed.
1.1.2
Organization and business model
ABC Fashion is a medium-sized direct-to-consumer online fashion retailer in this case. It
works with garment suppliers, a payment provider, and a shipping provider. Seasonal col-
lections and unsold stock influence purchasing and reporting. The proposed information
system supports sales and internal coordination but does not itself manufacture goods,
move parcels, or settle payments.
1.1.3
Products and target customers
The merchandise includes clothing, dresses, and accessories.
A product design has a
product identifier; a specific color-size combination has its own stock-keeping unit (SKU).
A white shirt in size M and a black shirt in size L therefore represent different variants
for inventory and order processing. Target customers are online shoppers who depend on
reliable photographs, size charts, product availability, and an accessible exchange process.
Internal users need the same variant identity to handle orders and stock accurately.

1.2
Review of Current Business Operations
1.2.1
Main activities and existing workflow
The business investigation describes collection and variant management, customer in-
formation, search and product viewing, size support, carts, orders, payment, picking,
delivery, exchanges, reviews, and management reporting. The current workflow moves
from product information to customer selection, an availability check, order processing,
picking, shipment, and after-sales support. Inventory is still handled with general product
codes and manual consolidation. Reserving inventory by SKU and generating SKU-level
pick lists are proposed capabilities; they must not be mistaken for features of the current
operation.
1.2.2
Participating departments and external parties
Administrators maintain catalog data. Merchandising staff plan collections; sales staff
handle orders; warehouse staff count, pick, pack, and receive returned items. Customer
service addresses questions and exchanges. Marketing uses product and sales informa-
tion, while managers evaluate performance. Customers, garment suppliers, the payment
provider, and the shipping provider participate from outside the organization. A stake-
holder need not be a direct software actor: this distinction becomes important in Chapter
2.

1.6
Proposed Information System
1.6.1
Purpose, objectives, and scope
The system should unify fashion inventory by SKU, support customer selection, connect
orders to warehouse work, and provide reliable management information. Its objectives
are to limit orders for unavailable variants, assist sizing decisions where specified, im-
prove picking accuracy, track delivery and returns, and consolidate sales and inventory
information.
The functional scope follows FR01–FR20 of the requirements specification: accounts
and profiles; categories, products and variants; text, voice and image search; product
details; cart and checkout; payments and refunds; SKU pick lists, inventory and returns;
shipping; comments; and reports.

1.6.4
Six business objectives
1. Synchronize inventory by color-size SKU. The investigation proposes reducing
unavailable-stock orders by 90%; this is a target, not an observed result, and requires
a baseline.
2. Offer measurement-based sizing assistance to reduce size-related returns. The concept
appears in the business scope and AI illustration; its acceptance criteria need a separate
FR.
3. Generate unambiguous SKU pick lists and support barcode checking to reduce wrong-
color and wrong-size fulfillment.
4. Standardize digital color codes and lookbook photography; display software alone can-
not guarantee the appearance of physical goods on all devices.
5. Coordinate size-exchange requests and reverse delivery with the shipping provider.
Return conditions still require agreement.
6. Analyze sales by collection, color, and size to plan replenishment and reduce stagnant
stock.

1.7.2
Associated business rules
1. Variant identity: a cart or order line must identify a SKU and its color and size,
not merely a product design.
2. Availability: an order must not be confirmed when a selected SKU cannot satisfy the
requested quantity. Checking and reserving stock must be consistent under concurrent
orders.
3. Order total: the amount derives from quantities and line prices; shipping, promo-
tions, and adjustments still need precise rules.
4. Payment state: choosing cash on delivery (COD) does not mean that money has
already been collected.
5. Returns: an incoming item is associated with its original SKU; inspection determines
whether it can re-enter saleable stock.
The requirement catalogue does not settle when stock is reserved and deducted, how failed
payments release reservations, or exactly how COD is represented. Chapter 2 therefore
makes these open questions explicit rather than silently assigning a policy.
1.8
Non-Functional Requirements
Ten NFR identifiers are used consistently. The figures of two seconds, 99.9%, and 5,000
are proposed targets from the requirements, not measured results. Test conditions must
be agreed before acceptance.
NFR01 – Performance
Normal product searches by text, voice, or image should return in two seconds
according to the requirements specification. The meaning of peak load, dataset
size, system configuration, and timing boundary remains open.
NFR02 – Security
Only authenticated and authorized users may access personal order information.
Tests should include unauthenticated requests and attempts to read another cus-
tomer’s order.
NFR03 – Usability
Customer pages should use consistent navigation and legible color/size labels.
Color fidelity requires accepted photography and display conditions; software
alone cannot guarantee identical rendering on every screen.
NFR04 – Availability
Target availability is at least 99.9% during business hours. The operating window,
measurement period, and treatment of maintenance must be defined.
13
NFR05 – Reliability
Confirmed orders and their stock effects must remain consistent and retrievable,
including after an interrupted transaction.
NFR06 – Scalability
The requirement catalogue refers to 5,000 “active users and SKUs.” Users and
SKUs are different quantities; the concurrency level and variant count must be
specified separately.
NFR07 – Maintainability
Changes to one functional area should be testable without uncontrolled effects
on others. The modular architecture mentioned in the earlier specification is a
later design decision.
NFR08 – Data integrity
Prevent confirmation of orders with invalid SKUs or insufficient stock; this con-
strains FR09 and FR16.
NFR09 – Compatibility
Support suitable desktop and mobile browsers; named versions and viewport sizes
remain to be agreed.
NFR10 – Compliance
Handle customer data in accordance with the project’s applicable com-
merce/privacy rules.
The requirement catalogue does not identify the exact
legal criteria, so compliance cannot yet be certified.

This chapter develops the requirements established in Chapter 1 into system actors, Use
Case descriptions, Activity Diagrams, Swimlane Diagrams, requirement traceability, and

2.1
Actor Analysis
An actor represents an external role that directly interacts with the proposed system.
The six actors in the ABC Fashion Use Case diagram are identified below.
1. Customer: Interacts through the web or mobile interface to register, log in, search
by keyword, voice or image, view products, add variants to the cart, check out, pay,
and submit comments or feedback.
2. Administrator: Manages product categories, products, and variant information.
3. Warehouse Staff: Views pick-lists, picks and packs items by SKU, updates inventory,
and processes returned items.
4. Manager: Accesses analytics dashboards, sales reports, inventory reports, and return
reports.
5. Payment Service: External service that processes online payments, verifies payment
status, and executes refunds.
6. Shipping Provider: External service that creates waybills and updates delivery and
exchange/return statuses.
The garment supplier is a stakeholder in the preliminary context diagram from Chapter
1. However, it is not drawn as a direct actor in the supplied Use Case diagram.

2.7
Requirement Validation
Requirement validation evaluates whether the requirements are clear, consistent, com-
plete, and testable against business rules. FR05, FR10, FR16, NFR01 and NFR08 are
suitable starting points for verification, subject to defined test conditions.
Problematic
wording
Revised requirement wording
“The system should be
fast.”
NFR01: The system shall return normal product-search results
(keyword, voice, image) within 2 seconds under peak operating
conditions. Peak load and test data still need to be defined.
“The system should be
user-friendly.”
NFR03: The system shall provide consistent navigation menus
across customer-facing product pages. A checklist must define what
consistency means.
“The system must
protect user data.”
NFR02: Only authenticated and authorized users shall access
their personal order and payment details via secured authentication
mechanisms. Verification must include access-control tests.
